# Where Experts Disagree

## 1. Consistency vs. Selective Publishing

### Expert A: Justin Welsh

Justin Welsh argues that consistency is the foundation of LinkedIn growth. He emphasizes publishing regularly, even when engagement is low, because audience growth is rarely linear. According to him, most creators fail because they stop posting before consistency has time to compound.

**Source:** Justin Welsh – "Building success through consistency on LinkedIn"  
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_prior-to-2019-id-never-used-social-media-activity-7417413059574546432-g6k8/

### Expert B: Richard van der Blom

Richard van der Blom believes that publishing frequently is less important than building authority. He argues that many creators optimize for impressions and follower growth, while B2B buyers care more about expertise, credibility, and relevance. A smaller number of high-quality posts aimed at the right audience can outperform frequent posting with little strategic value.

**Source:** Richard van der Blom – "Authority Beats Attention"

### My Position

I believe both consistency and quality matter, but if I had to prioritize one, I would choose Richard van der Blom's approach. Consistency is valuable only when it consistently delivers useful insights. Publishing simply to maintain a schedule can reduce perceived expertise, while fewer high-quality posts are more likely to build trust with decision-makers in B2B SaaS.

## 2. Content Distribution vs. Forwardable Content

### Expert A: Ross Simmonds

Ross Simmonds argues that marketers should maximize the value of every piece of content through systematic distribution. A single webinar, article, or podcast should be repurposed into LinkedIn posts, newsletters, videos, and other formats to increase reach without significantly increasing production effort.

**Source:** Ross Simmonds – "Content Distribution vs. Content Creation"  
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/rosssimmonds_marketing-activity-7378152775156711425-pNyg/

### Expert B: Amanda Natividad

Amanda Natividad focuses less on distribution channels and more on creating content that people naturally share inside their organizations. She argues that the best B2B content is not simply clickable—it is forwardable. Buyers share content that helps them explain ideas, support decisions, or influence colleagues.

**Source:** Amanda Natividad – "The Best B2B Content Gets Forwarded"

### My Position

I believe Amanda's perspective should come first. Distribution can amplify content, but it cannot compensate for weak ideas. If a piece of content is valuable enough to be shared internally, repurposing it across multiple channels becomes much more effective. Quality should precede distribution.

## 3. Storytelling vs. Educational Authority

### Expert A: Shaan Puri

Shaan Puri believes storytelling is one of the most powerful tools in marketing. He recommends using emotion, conflict, transformation, and relatable experiences to capture attention and make ideas memorable. According to him, people connect more deeply with stories than with facts alone.

**Source:** Shaan Puri – "Storytelling Masterclass"  
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z2BnqYArwaw

### Expert B: Julian Shapiro

Julian Shapiro argues that long-form educational content builds authority because it demonstrates expertise. Even if readers do not consume every section, the depth of the content itself signals credibility and increases trust among potential buyers.

**Source:** Julian Shapiro Interview  
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1RVflXVtEgc

### My Position

I do not see these approaches as mutually exclusive. Storytelling is highly effective for attracting attention, while educational content builds long-term credibility. For B2B SaaS companies, the strongest strategy is to combine both: use storytelling to engage readers initially, then support the narrative with practical insights and evidence that reinforce authority.
